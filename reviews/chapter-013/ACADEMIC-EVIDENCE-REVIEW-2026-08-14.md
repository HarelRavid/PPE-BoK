# Chapter 13 — Academic / Evidence Review

**Record date:** 2026-08-14  
**Chapter:** 013 — Polyethylene (PE)  
**PDS baseline:** 1.0  
**Review type:** Academic / Evidence & Objectivity Review  
**Decision:** PASS for the current core materials/mechanisms claim set, with stated applicability limits

## 1. Review purpose

This review checks whether the principal non-normative scientific claims used in Chapter 13 are supported by directly reviewed primary research rather than by generated summaries, unsupported engineering intuition or standards being asked to carry claims outside their scope.

The review is deliberately separate from Standards Validation. Academic literature supports material mechanisms and engineering interpretation; it does not replace the governing ISO product, classification, test or application standard.

## 2. Primary research set reviewed

### AE-013-01 — Brown, Donofrio & Lu (1987)

**Citation:** N. Brown, J. Donofrio, X. Lu, “The transition between ductile and slow-crack-growth failure in polyethylene,” *Polymer*, 28(8), 1326–1330 (1987). DOI: `10.1016/0032-3861(87)90446-0`.

**Direct support:** Under long-time constant stress, polyethylene can exhibit ductile creep-controlled failure and brittle slow-crack-growth failure; the transition depends on the kinetics/material parameters and the crack/defect condition.

**Chapter relevance:** Supports Investigation 4's distinction between creep/ductile long-time behaviour and SCG-dominated long-time fracture, and supports the warning that the governing mechanism can change with stress/time conditions.

**Applicability boundary:** The paper supports the mechanism distinction. It does not establish a universal project transition stress/time or an ISO design value.

### AE-013-02 — Huang & Brown (1991)

**Citation:** Y.-L. Huang, N. Brown, “Dependence of slow crack growth in polyethylene on butyl branch density: Morphology and theory,” *Journal of Polymer Science Part B: Polymer Physics*, 29(1), 129–137 (1991). DOI: `10.1002/polb.1991.090290116`.

**Direct support:** Experimental morphology measurements and modelling relate branch density, lamellar parameters and tie-molecule population to slow crack growth behaviour in an ethylene-hexene polyethylene system.

**Chapter relevance:** Supports Investigations 2–4: molecular architecture/morphology matter to crack resistance; short-chain branching/tie-molecule concepts cannot be replaced by short-term strength alone.

**Applicability boundary:** The tested copolymer/model is evidence for a mechanism, not a universal quantitative SCG correlation for every PE100 compound.

### AE-013-03 — Lu, Zhou & Brown (1997)

**Citation:** X. Lu, Z. Zhou, N. Brown, “A sensitive mechanical test for slow crack growth in polyethylene,” *Polymer Engineering & Science*, 37(11), 1896–1900 (1997). DOI: `10.1002/pen.11839`.

**Direct support:** A notched constant-load PENT-type test was shown to be sensitive to molecular structure and morphology; SCG resistance was linked to tie-molecule density and crystal strength.

**Chapter relevance:** Supports the chapter's separation of global pressure loading from local notch/crack-growth resistance and the statement that SCG qualification is mechanism-specific evidence.

**Applicability boundary:** A specimen-level accelerated test is not proof that an installed system tolerates arbitrary gouges, poor joints, restraint loads or unqualified environments.

### AE-013-04 — Men, Rieger, Enderle & Lilge (2004)

**Citation:** Y. F. Men, J. Rieger, H.-F. Enderle, D. Lilge, “The mobility of the amorphous phase in polyethylene as a determining factor for slow crack growth,” *European Physical Journal E*, 15(4), 421–425 (2004). DOI: `10.1140/epje/i2004-10059-3`.

**Direct support:** The study connects PE pipe SCG to craze growth/fibril rupture and shows that amorphous-phase mobility can control craze-fibril development; DMA-derived mobility correlated with FNCT-derived lifetimes for the materials studied.

**Chapter relevance:** Supports Investigation 2's claim that semicrystalline PE behaviour depends on both crystalline and amorphous structure and supports the chapter's craze/SCG mechanism discussion.

**Applicability boundary:** The correlation is material/test specific and is not a substitute for the product-standard qualification route.

### AE-013-05 — Pan et al. (2015)

**Citation:** Y. Pan, X. Gao, Z. Wang, J. Lei, Z. Li, K. Shen, “Effect of different morphologies on slow crack growth of high-density polyethylene,” *RSC Advances*, 5, 28191–28202 (2015). DOI: `10.1039/C5RA00918A`.

**Direct support:** HDPE specimens prepared with different morphologies showed materially different SCG behaviour, providing experimental evidence that morphology/orientation/crystalline structure influence crack-growth resistance.

**Chapter relevance:** Supports the general structure → morphology → engineering-response chain in Investigations 2–4 and the statement that processing history can affect engineering behaviour.

**Applicability boundary:** The special specimen-processing route is not representative of every extruded pressure pipe and shall not be used to assign quantitative pipe design values.

### AE-013-06 — Pilz et al. (2022)

**Citation:** G. Pilz, S. Wurzer, M. Morak, G. Pinter, “Assessment of the stepped isothermal method for accelerated creep testing of high-density polyethylene,” *Mechanics of Time-Dependent Materials*, 26, 775–790 (2022). DOI: `10.1007/s11043-021-09512-1`.

**Direct support:** Experimental HDPE creep testing demonstrated time- and temperature-dependent viscoelastic response, non-linearity at higher stress/temperature, and a distinct influence of thermal history/morphology on creep behaviour. Accelerated master-curve construction required method-specific care rather than arbitrary temperature shifting.

**Chapter relevance:** Strongly supports Investigations 2 and 4 and the warning in Investigation 5 that elevated temperature is an acceleration variable only within a validated methodology.

**Applicability boundary:** The tested injection-moulded HDPE grade and SIM method are not the ISO 9080 pipe-form qualification method and are not used to generate Chapter 13 design values.

### AE-013-07 — Kanters, Remerie & Govaert (2016)

**Citation:** M. J. W. Kanters, K. Remerie, L. E. Govaert, “A new protocol for accelerated screening of long-term plasticity-controlled failure of polyethylene pipe grades,” *Polymer Engineering & Science*, 56(6), 676–688 (2016). DOI: `10.1002/pen.24294`.

**Direct support:** A primary experimental study on a PE100 pipe grade developed and validated an accelerated approach for long-term plasticity-controlled failure using strain-rate/temperature-dependent material response.

**Chapter relevance:** Supports the central proposition that short-term strength alone is not an adequate long-term design story and that long-term failure depends on rate/temperature/time-dependent mechanisms.

**Applicability boundary:** The proposed accelerated protocol is research methodology, not the governing classification/product-standard method adopted by Chapter 13.

### AE-013-08 — Gao et al. (2024)

**Citation:** B. Gao, M. Luo, K. Lin, J. Ding, B. Liu, “Fatigue crack growth and slow crack growth of HDPE pipes under internal pressure and flat plate compression,” *International Journal of Pressure Vessels and Piping*, 208, 105155 (2024). DOI: `10.1016/j.ijpvp.2024.105155`.

**Direct support:** Experiments on pre-cracked HDPE pipe under pulsatile internal pressure and external compression showed load-ratio-dependent fatigue crack growth and a relationship between fatigue-crack-growth and slow-crack-growth kinetics for the conditions studied.

**Chapter relevance:** Supports the chapter's statement that cyclic/transient loading and local defects are additional engineering concerns that cannot be dismissed by a static nominal pressure calculation alone.

**Applicability boundary:** The specific pre-crack geometry, 23 °C test conditions and load histories do not establish a universal fatigue design curve or cycle limit for PE pressure piping.

## 4. Objectivity and overclaim check

### Passed controls

- The manuscript does not claim that one morphology variable uniquely predicts PE pipe life.
- SCG qualification evidence is not presented as immunity from arbitrary installation damage.
- Short-term mechanical behaviour is not substituted for long-term pressure qualification.
- The manuscript separates material behaviour from product/system qualification.
- Cyclic loading is treated as a trigger for additional assessment rather than assigned an unsupported universal fatigue limit.
- Failure appearance is treated as hypothesis-generating evidence rather than automatic root-cause proof.
- Academic literature is not used to override an applicable standard.

### Controlled caution

The manuscript's simplified phrases such as “crystalline regions and amorphous regions” are appropriate at Chapter 13 engineering depth, but deeper morphology, lamellae, spherulites, tie molecules, molecular-weight distribution and constitutive viscoelastic theory remain allocated to later dedicated polymer-science chapters in the recovered architecture.

## 5. Evidence gaps / intentional exclusions

The following are not treated as defects in Chapter 13 because they belong to other planned chapters, but they should not be implied as fully covered here:

- full polymer morphology science;
- detailed constitutive creep/viscoelastic modelling;
- fatigue-life design methodology;
- fracture-mechanics parameter development;
- chemical ageing/oxidation kinetics;
- detailed joining science and fusion-process qualification;
- installation-damage acceptance criteria;
- detailed failure-analysis laboratory methods.

These scopes remain in the book-wide knowledge map and shall receive dedicated treatment later.

**PASS — core non-normative material/mechanism claims in the current Chapter 13 manuscript have adequate primary-research support for this chapter's engineering depth.**
