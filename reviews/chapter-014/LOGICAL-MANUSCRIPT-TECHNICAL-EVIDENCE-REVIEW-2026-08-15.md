## 4. Investigation 1–8 technical continuity

### 4.1 Atomic / terminology model

The chapter uses atoms, elements, molecules, molecular entities and chemical substances at the minimum depth required for later bonding reasoning. It rejects a planetary-orbit literal model and does not require unnecessary quantum mathematics.

**Disposition:** PASS.

### 4.2 Primary bonding model

Covalent bonding, ionic character and extended electron delocalization are used as explanatory models rather than as direct bulk-property classifiers. The text appropriately rejects a pure ionic/covalent false dichotomy and avoids inventing a stand-alone metallic-bond authority where the verified evidence path is electron delocalization.

**Disposition:** PASS.

### 4.3 Noncovalent interactions

The chapter correctly treats van der Waals forces as an umbrella containing dipole–dipole, dipole-induced-dipole and London/dispersion interactions in the adopted IUPAC framework, and does not imply that dispersion disappears in polar systems. Hydrogen bonding remains separately controlled through the IUPAC 2011 Recommendation/Technical Report rather than being reduced to a universal 'strong dipole' rule.

**Disposition:** PASS, subject only to the already-recorded controlled title change to `FIG-014-002 — Primary Bonding and Noncovalent Interaction Map` during canonical integration.

### 4.4 Carbon / hybridization / sigma-pi treatment

`sp3`, `sp2` and `sp` are retained as local idealized explanatory models, with approximate reference geometry rather than exact engineering tolerances. Sigma/pi terminology is explicitly contextualized as a localized-bond teaching model and is not used to infer bulk polymer performance.

**Disposition:** PASS.

### 4.5 Ethene → polyethylene bridge

The manuscript distinguishes ethene from established industrial `ethylene` usage, distinguishes monomer / monomeric unit / CRU concepts, and labels `CH2=CH2 → [–CH2–CH2–]n` as a structural comparison rather than a polymerization mechanism. It also explicitly prevents the PE repeat-unit drawing from being interpreted as a PE80/PE100 or service qualification.

**Disposition:** PASS.

## 5. Investigation 9 — primary-evidence audit

### 5.1 Case A — PA6 + water

The retained claim is bounded to the studied PA6 systems: water exposure is associated with sorption/swelling and mechanical/molecular changes, with amide / hydrogen-bond interactions forming a defensible mechanism hypothesis and morphology/conditioning remaining important confounders.

The external publisher / primary-record recheck supports:

- the Shinzawa & Mizukado paper identity and DOI;
- wet-treatment / water-absorption effects on PA6 elastic response and intermolecular hydrogen-bond interpretation;
- the Sambale et al. paper identity and DOI;
- experimental water sorption and swelling of PA6 under controlled conditioning / geometry conditions.

No universal PA6 design reduction or compatibility limit is imported.

**Disposition:** PASS.

### 5.2 Case B — selected fluoropolymer membranes

The retained lesson is methodological: systematic membrane measurements across selected fluoropolymers demonstrate that simple fluorination / polarity / ether-group descriptors are insufficient to create a universal transport ranking.

The external primary/open-access record confirms that the study investigated mass transport of polar and non-polar gases through fluoropolymer membranes selected for different degrees of fluorination and ether-containing monomer structures.

No pipe-wall permeability value or piping-grade fluoropolymer ranking is transferred.

**Disposition:** PASS, with the already-recorded wording control to prefer `packing / morphology-related free-volume effects` over treating `free volume` as a directly established independent material state where the specific source does not measure it directly.

### 5.3 Case C — PFA cooling history / morphology

The Wiley primary record confirms that the study measured permeability, diffusion and solubility coefficients for H2, N2 and O2 in PFA/PTFE-copolymer specimens prepared using faster and slower cooling routes, and that the slow-cooled specimens had markedly higher permeation resistance. The authors explicitly concluded that process can be as important as polymer grade for the measured PFA permeation response.

The chapter correctly uses this only to demonstrate processing/morphology sensitivity and does not create a universal cooling prescription, pipe-wall allowance or hydrogen-service qualification.

**Disposition:** PASS.
