# CDB-014 — Chapter Design Brief: Atomic Structure, Chemical Bonding and Carbon Chemistry for Polymer Engineers

## 1. Chapter purpose

Develop the first dedicated first-principles science chapter in the recovered PPE-BoK polymer-science sequence.

The chapter shall give a practicing piping engineer enough atomic, bonding and carbon-chemistry understanding to explain why polymer structures differ and why those differences can produce different thermal, mechanical, chemical, transport and joining behaviour.

The chapter is **not** intended to become a general chemistry textbook. Every scientific concept shall be selected because it supports later engineering judgement in plastic piping.

The chapter shall deepen material that is only introduced at high level in Chapter 009 without duplicating Chapter 009's broad polymer-engineering role. It shall establish the scientific foundation required for Working Chapters 015–020.

## 2. Primary audience

Practicing mechanical, process, piping, materials, reliability, inspection and owner/EPC engineers who use polymeric piping but may not have formal polymer-chemistry training.

Secondary readers include junior engineers, inspectors, joining specialists, manufacturers and advanced technical students who need a first-principles bridge from chemistry to engineering behaviour.

## 3. Engineering problem addressed

Why can materials that are all described as “plastics” behave very differently in pressure piping, chemical service, temperature exposure, permeation, cracking and fusion joining?

The chapter shall show how an engineer moves from:

`atomic structure → bonding → molecular geometry / polarity / intermolecular interaction → plausible material mechanism → measured/qualified engineering property → engineering decision`

The chapter shall also teach the boundary of that reasoning: molecular chemistry can explain mechanisms and trends, but it does not by itself establish pressure rating, allowable temperature, chemical compatibility, lifetime or system qualification.

## 4. Reader outcomes

After completing the chapter, the reader should be able to:

1. Explain the engineering relevance of atoms, electrons, valence electrons and molecular structure without relying on unnecessary quantum-mechanics detail.
2. Distinguish ionic, covalent and metallic primary bonding from hydrogen bonding, dipole interactions and London/van der Waals interactions.
3. Explain why bond type, bond polarity and intermolecular forces influence properties such as stiffness, molecular mobility, melting/softening behaviour, chemical interaction and diffusion/permeation.
4. Explain carbon tetravalency and why carbon can form chains, branches, rings and multiple bonds that underpin polymer chemistry.
5. Explain the engineering meaning of `sp`, `sp2` and `sp3` hybridization at the level required to understand molecular geometry and carbon–carbon single/double bonds.
6. Distinguish sigma and pi bonding sufficiently to understand why the carbon–carbon double bond in ethylene is chemically different from the carbon–carbon single bonds in a polyethylene backbone.
7. Read a simple polymer or monomer structural formula and identify features that may influence polarity, rotational freedom, intermolecular attraction and molecular packing.
8. Use PE, PP, PVC/CPVC, PVDF/PTFE and selected polar polymers only as controlled illustrations of structure–property reasoning, without turning the chapter into a material-selection chapter.
9. Explain why a molecular-level observation is a mechanism hypothesis, not a project design value.
10. Identify which later PPE-BoK chapter owns the next engineering question: polymerization, chain architecture, morphology, thermal behaviour, viscoelasticity, fracture/degradation or material-family qualification.

## 5. Scientific / engineering input variables to be addressed

This foundational chapter does not use a project Design Basis in the same way as a pressure-design chapter. Its controlled scientific inputs shall include, where relevant:

- atomic number and elemental identity;
- electron shells / orbitals only to the depth required for bonding;
- valence-electron count;
- electronegativity and bond polarity;
- bond type;
- bond order;
- approximate bond geometry;
- bond rotation constraints;
- molecular symmetry where useful;
- permanent dipoles;
- hydrogen-bond donor/acceptor capability where applicable;
- London/dispersion interactions;
- carbon hybridization state;
- carbon–carbon single and double bonds;
- simple monomer and repeat-unit structures;
- molecular features that may affect packing, mobility or intermolecular attraction.

The chapter shall repeatedly distinguish these molecular descriptors from measured engineering properties and from qualified product/system performance.

## 6. Standards and evidence framework

No pressure-piping product or design standard governs the atomic/bonding science taught in this chapter. The standards layer is therefore primarily terminology, notation and controlled cross-reference rather than design acceptance.

The initial evidence path shall include:

- **ISO 472:2013 + Amendment 1:2018 — Plastics — Vocabulary** for controlled plastics/polymer terminology where applicable. ISO currently lists the 2013 edition as published but under revision; status shall be rechecked before publication.
- **ISO 1043-1:2011 + Amendment 1:2016 — Plastics — Symbols and abbreviated terms — Part 1** where standardized polymer abbreviations are used. Current lifecycle status shall be rechecked before publication.
- **IUPAC Compendium of Chemical Terminology (Gold Book)** for chemical terminology and definitions, with version/date captured when terminology is used.
- Applicable IUPAC nomenclature recommendations where chemical naming requires formal control.
- Authoritative chemistry / polymer-science academic sources for the scientific mechanisms developed in the chapter.
- Primary literature where a claim moves beyond stable textbook-level science into a material-specific or engineering-performance mechanism.

### 6.1 Standards-validation rule

This chapter shall not manufacture normative engineering requirements from vocabulary documents.

Exact standards-derived terminology, definitions, symbols or abbreviations used in final text shall be checked against the authoritative current source during Standards Validation. If ISO 472 or ISO 1043-1 is revised or withdrawn before publication, the chapter shall be rechecked for terminology impact.

### 6.2 Evidence boundary

Stable foundational chemistry may be supported by authoritative reference works and established academic texts. Material-specific claims about property magnitude, long-term behaviour, chemical resistance, permeability, fracture, pressure capability or joining performance require appropriate material-specific evidence and normally belong to later chapters.

## 7. Core scientific relationships / quantitative treatment

This chapter is primarily mechanism-based rather than calculation-driven. It shall use quantitative relationships only where they materially improve engineering understanding.

Candidates to evaluate during Technical Outline include:

1. Electronegativity difference as a qualitative indicator of bond polarity, with explicit warning against treating it as a universal engineering-property predictor.
2. Simple molecular-geometry relationships only where needed to explain `sp`, `sp2` and `sp3` structures.
3. Energy concepts such as bond dissociation energy only as bounded comparative concepts; no unsupported direct conversion to material strength.
4. Dipole moment / molecular polarity concepts only to the depth needed to explain intermolecular interactions.

No equation quota is required. Every retained equation shall include symbols, units, assumptions, validity limits and an explicit statement of what engineering conclusion it does **not** establish.

## 9. Investigation roadmap

The exact headings may be refined during Technical Outline, but the chapter should progress approximately through:

### Investigation 1 — Why should a piping engineer care about atoms and bonds?

Establish the engineering problem and connect molecular science to later piping decisions.

### Investigation 2 — What is matter made of at the useful engineering scale?

Atoms, elements, molecules and compounds; distinguish useful engineering abstraction from unnecessary subatomic detail.

### Investigation 3 — Which electrons actually control chemical bonding?

Valence electrons, shells/orbitals and the minimum periodic trends required to reason about polymer chemistry.

### Investigation 4 — What are primary bonds and why do material classes differ?

Ionic, covalent and metallic bonding; controlled comparison of metals, ceramics and polymers without oversimplifying bulk behaviour.

### Investigation 5 — What holds polymer molecules together when they are not covalently bonded to each other?

Hydrogen bonding, dipole interactions and London/van der Waals forces; distinguish intramolecular from intermolecular bonding.

### Investigation 6 — Why is carbon uniquely useful for polymer backbones?

Tetravalency, carbon–carbon bonding, chains, branches, rings and structural diversity.

### Investigation 7 — What do `sp`, `sp2`, `sp3`, sigma and pi bonds mean to the engineer?

Geometry, rotational freedom and single versus double bonds at an engineering-use depth.

### Investigation 8 — What is special about ethylene?

C=C double bond, molecular geometry and the bridge to polymerization; stop before catalyst/reaction-mechanism treatment owned by Chapter 015.

### Investigation 9 — How do molecular features become engineering-property hypotheses?

Controlled examples involving polarity, intermolecular attraction, packing and mobility; connect to stiffness, thermal behaviour, diffusion/permeation, chemical interaction and joining only at mechanism level.

### Investigation 10 — What can chemistry tell us, and where must the engineer stop?

Explicit decision boundary between first-principles reasoning and qualified engineering evidence. End with the downstream chapter map.

## 10. Common errors the chapter must explicitly address

- Treating all plastics as mechanically similar because they are all polymers.
- Treating covalent bond strength as equivalent to bulk tensile strength or pipe pressure capability.
- Treating molecular polarity as a complete chemical-compatibility rule.
- Treating high bond energy as proof of high service temperature.
- Treating intermolecular forces and intramolecular bonds as the same thing.
- Confusing a monomer chemical formula with the structure/properties of the final compounded piping material.
- Assuming `sp`, `sp2` or `sp3` labels by themselves determine macroscopic performance.
- Assuming a chemical structure can replace material testing, product qualification or application standards.
- Teaching quantum chemistry detail that does not change an engineering decision.
- Duplicating polymerization, morphology, viscoelasticity or fracture content that belongs to later chapters.

## 11. Out of scope / controlled cross-reference

The chapter shall introduce but not duplicate full treatments of:

- polymerization mechanisms, catalysts and process–structure effects — Working Chapter 015;
- molecular weight, branching, crosslinking and chain architecture — Working Chapter 016;
- crystallinity, lamellae, spherulites, amorphous regions and tie molecules — Working Chapter 017;
- `Tg`, `Tm`, thermal expansion and thermophysical design behaviour — Working Chapter 018;
- viscoelasticity, creep, stress relaxation and time–temperature superposition — Working Chapter 019;
- fracture, SCG, RCP, fatigue, ESC, oxidation, UV ageing and permeation engineering — Working Chapter 020;
- detailed material-family selection — Chapters 021 onward;
- pressure rating, MRS, SDR and product/system qualification — Chapters 012–013 and later design/application chapters;
- detailed joining physics and procedures — Part VII;
- chemical-resistance database use — material-selection/application chapters.

Chapter 009 remains the broad polymer-fundamentals foundation. Chapter 014 shall deepen the recovered first-principles chemistry sequence rather than repackage Chapter 009.

## 12. Technical style and depth rules

1. Start each Investigation from an engineering question rather than an academic chemistry syllabus.
2. Use molecular drawings and controlled analogies aggressively where they improve understanding.
3. Do not introduce quantum numbers, wave functions or orbital mathematics unless a later engineering conclusion genuinely requires them.
4. Distinguish **mechanism explanation** from **engineering qualification** on every structure–property bridge.
5. Use common piping polymers as examples, not as unsourced ranking tables.
6. Any material-specific quantitative property shall be sourced and bounded or deferred to the owning material chapter.
7. Preserve scientific accuracy even when simplifying; simplified models shall be labeled as such.
8. Avoid anthropomorphic language such as molecules “wanting” or atoms “trying” to bond except where explicitly identified as a teaching analogy.
