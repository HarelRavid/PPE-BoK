# Chapter 4 — Defining Engineering Requirements

## Why This Chapter Matters

The quality of an engineering solution is limited by the quality of the requirements on which it is based. A sophisticated design built on incomplete or incorrect requirements remains inadequate.

Many piping failures originate before material selection or calculation, when important operating conditions, maintenance needs, environmental factors, or future changes are omitted.

## 4.1 What Engineering Requirements Are

Engineering requirements describe what the piping system must accomplish throughout its intended life. They define the boundary conditions for design, procurement, construction, testing, operation, inspection, and maintenance.

Requirements should describe required performance rather than prematurely prescribe a solution.

> “The system shall transport 25 m³/h of 30 wt.% sodium hydroxide between 20°C and 65°C without unacceptable leakage over the defined design life.”

is a requirement.

> “The system shall be PE100.”

is a design choice that must be justified against the requirement.

## 4.2 Sources of Requirements

Requirements may originate from:

- process and production needs;
- laws, permits, and regulatory obligations;
- engineering codes and standards;
- client and corporate specifications;
- process-safety studies;
- environmental constraints;
- operations and maintenance philosophy;
- quality and inspection strategy;
- previous failures and lessons learned;
- procurement, space, and schedule constraints.

Conflicts among requirement sources must be resolved explicitly.

## 4.3 Functional Requirements

Functional requirements define what the system must do, for example:

- transport the required medium and flow;
- isolate equipment safely;
- permit draining, venting, flushing, and sampling;
- prevent unacceptable leakage or contamination;
- support start-up, shutdown, cleaning, and maintenance;
- allow defined future expansion.

Functional requirements should be measurable or verifiable wherever practical.

## 4.4 Performance Requirements

Performance requirements define how well the system must perform. They may include:

- design pressure and temperature;
- required flow and acceptable pressure loss;
- leakage criteria;
- design life and availability;
- allowable movement or deflection;
- cleanliness or purity limits;
- inspection and maintenance intervals;
- reliability or containment targets.

## 4.5 Environmental Requirements

The environment can govern long-term performance. Relevant conditions may include:

- minimum and maximum ambient temperatures;
- solar and ultraviolet exposure;
- rain, humidity, marine atmosphere, or chemical vapors;
- burial, groundwater, soil movement, or traffic loads;
- wind, seismic, snow, and ice loads;
- indoor fire exposure or accidental impact;
- accessibility for inspection and repair.

## 4.6 Operational Requirements

Requirements must reflect how the plant will actually operate:

- continuous, intermittent, or batch service;
- start-up and shutdown frequency;
- pressure and temperature cycles;
- cleaning and sanitation procedures;
- pump and control-valve behavior;
- process interruptions and emergency states;
- expected production increases or composition changes.

Cumulative cycles may be more important than a nominal operating point.

## 4.7 Maintenance and Inspection Requirements

Design should account for:

- access for inspection and repair;
- replaceability of valves and instruments;
- isolation and drainage;
- availability of fusion equipment and qualified personnel;
- spare parts and transition components;
- calibration and testing needs;
- documentation and traceability;
- acceptable downtime.

A low-cost installation can become an expensive asset if maintainability is ignored.

## 4.8 Safety Requirements

Safety extends beyond pressure containment. Depending on the service, requirements may address:

- toxic or corrosive release;
- fire and flammability;
- static electricity;
- personnel exposure;
- impact and mechanical damage;
- emergency isolation;
- secondary containment;
- environmental release;
- stored energy during testing or operation.

## 4.9 Change and Traceability

Requirements evolve. Production targets, chemicals, equipment, regulations, and operating philosophy can change.

Every significant engineering decision should be traceable to one or more approved requirements. Traceability supports design review, management of change, commissioning, and later failure investigation.

| Requirement | Typical engineering consequence |
|---|---|
| Chemical compatibility | Material and joint selection |
| Maximum pressure and temperature | Pressure-temperature rating |
| Outdoor installation | UV and thermal-movement provisions |
| Frequent maintenance | Isolation and connection strategy |
| Abrasive slurry | Velocity, wall, geometry, and inspection |

## 4.10 Common Mistakes

- beginning detailed design before requirements are complete;
- confusing solutions with requirements;
- omitting transients, cleaning, or maintenance conditions;
- failing to document assumptions;
- treating client preference as technical justification;
- not updating requirements after project changes;
- failing to define how acceptance will be verified.

## Chapter Summary

Engineering requirements define the problem the piping system must solve. They must be complete, measurable where practical, traceable, and maintained through project change.

Poor requirements cannot be corrected by excellent execution later. Clear requirements improve every subsequent decision from material selection through failure investigation.
