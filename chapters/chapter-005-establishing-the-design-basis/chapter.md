# Chapter 5 — Establishing the Design Basis

## Why This Chapter Matters

Every successful engineering project begins with a common technical understanding of the system being designed. This understanding is formalized in the Design Basis.

For plastic piping, the Design Basis must define more than nominal pressure, temperature, and flow. It must also document assumptions, uncertainty, operating philosophy, environmental exposure, cyclic service, maintenance strategy, and foreseeable changes that can affect long-term performance.

## 5.1 Definition

The Design Basis is the documented description of the conditions, assumptions, constraints, codes, and performance requirements that define the intended service of a piping system.

It is the technical foundation for design, procurement, construction, testing, commissioning, operation, maintenance, and modification.

The central question it answers is:

> What system are we actually designing, and under which defined conditions must it remain fit for service?

## 5.2 More Than Nominal Process Data

Nominal pressure, temperature, and flow are essential, but insufficient. A complete Design Basis may also include:

- process-fluid composition and variability;
- normal, minimum, maximum, and transient conditions;
- start-up, shutdown, flushing, and cleaning;
- design life and availability expectations;
- pressure and temperature cycling;
- external environment and installation category;
- mechanical and equipment loads;
- inspection and testing philosophy;
- maintenance and repair strategy;
- future expansion and management of change;
- applicable laws, codes, standards, and specifications.

## 5.3 Operating Conditions and Design Conditions

Operating conditions describe expected service. Design conditions define the envelope the system must safely withstand in accordance with the governing design method and project philosophy.

A system may normally operate at 6 barg and 35°C while requiring assessment at a higher pressure or temperature because of equipment shutoff, control behavior, static head, upset conditions, or code requirements.

Design conditions should not be created by arbitrary margins. Their basis must be documented and consistent with the applicable code and credible process scenarios.

## 5.4 The Design Envelope

A piping system should be evaluated against an envelope rather than one operating point. Relevant dimensions can include:

- pressure;
- temperature;
- flow and velocity;
- chemical concentration and impurities;
- phase distribution;
- time at each condition;
- cycle count and transient rate;
- external temperature and radiation;
- mechanical loading and support movement.

For thermoplastics, time and temperature are especially important because strength, stiffness, creep, and chemical response can change substantially across the envelope.

## 5.5 Explicit Assumptions

All designs rely on assumptions, for example:

- production will not exceed a stated capacity;
- fluid concentration remains within a stated range;
- supports will be installed and maintained as designed;
- inspection intervals will be followed;
- transient events occur no more frequently than specified;
- cleaning chemicals and temperatures remain controlled.

Assumptions are not defects. Hidden assumptions are. They must be documented so they can be reviewed and revised when conditions change.

## 5.6 Applicable Requirements and Precedence

The Design Basis should identify:

- governing law and regulatory authority;
- applicable design codes and product standards;
- project and client specifications;
- manufacturer limits where relevant;
- conflict and precedence rules;
- required deviations or engineering justifications.

A product standard, design code, and installation guideline do not necessarily serve the same purpose. Their roles must be distinguished.

## 5.7 A Living Engineering Document

Facilities change. Pumps are replaced, production increases, chemicals change, repairs alter geometry, and control strategies evolve.

The Design Basis should therefore be reviewed through management of change whenever modifications affect the service envelope or system assumptions.

An outdated Design Basis creates false confidence because later decisions may be technically consistent with conditions that no longer exist.

## 5.8 Consequences of an Incomplete Design Basis

Typical examples include:

- selecting material for normal process temperature while ignoring hot cleaning cycles;
- checking static pressure but omitting frequent pressure cycling;
- designing supports without considering thermal movement;
- assessing pure-chemical compatibility but overlooking contaminants;
- assuming single-phase flow where gas evolves or solids precipitate;
- specifying pressure testing without considering trapped air, viscoelastic response, or test temperature;
- failing to provide access for inspection or repair.

## 5.9 Minimum Design-Basis Content

A practical Design Basis should normally define:

1. system function and boundaries;
2. fluid composition and physical state;
3. operating and design envelopes;
4. transient and abnormal scenarios;
5. required design life;
6. codes, standards, and specifications;
7. material and cleanliness constraints;
8. hydraulic and mechanical assumptions;
9. installation environment;
10. inspection, testing, and acceptance philosophy;
11. operation, maintenance, and repair assumptions;
12. documented uncertainties and open actions.

## Chapter Summary

The Design Basis converts process intent and project requirements into a controlled engineering foundation. It defines the service envelope, assumptions, constraints, and governing requirements against which all later decisions are evaluated.

A complete and maintained Design Basis reduces inconsistency, supports review and change management, and prevents failures rooted in incorrect or incomplete assumptions.
