# Chapter 6 — Service Conditions and the Design Envelope

## Why This Chapter Matters

Industrial systems rarely operate at a single pressure, temperature, or flow rate. They experience variation caused by production changes, equipment operation, maintenance, environment, control actions, and process disturbances.

Designing for one nominal point can create a system that performs well during ideal operation but becomes unreliable during ordinary transients or long-term exposure.

## 6.1 Service Conditions

Service conditions are the complete set of physical, chemical, mechanical, operational, temporal, and environmental conditions that can act on a piping system during its life.

They may include:

- all process fluids and phases;
- pressure and temperature ranges;
- flow and transient characteristics;
- chemical composition and concentration;
- internal and external mechanical loads;
- installation and environmental exposure;
- cleaning, maintenance, and temporary service;
- duration, cycle count, and design life;
- foreseeable abnormal events.

Together they form the design envelope.

## 6.2 Nominal Operation Is Not the Envelope

Nominal values from a PFD or data sheet are useful but rarely define the most demanding state. Assessment should consider:

- minimum and maximum throughput;
- pump start, stop, and trip;
- valve opening and closure;
- start-up and shutdown;
- batch changes;
- cleaning, flushing, and draining;
- blocked outlet or deadhead where credible;
- utility loss and emergency isolation;
- ambient extremes.

The governing condition may be a short transient, a recurring cycle, or prolonged moderate exposure rather than the highest steady value.

## 6.3 Mechanical Service Conditions

Loads may arise from:

- internal and external pressure;
- vacuum;
- thermal expansion and contraction;
- pipe and fluid weight;
- valves, instruments, hoses, and connected equipment;
- support settlement or misalignment;
- wind, seismic, snow, burial, and traffic;
- vibration and pulsation;
- accidental impact;
- construction and testing activities.

Loads must be considered in combination, not as isolated checks.

## 6.4 Chemical Service Conditions

Compatibility cannot be assessed from the primary chemical name alone. The engineer should define:

- concentration range;
- temperature and exposure duration;
- impurities and contaminants;
- dissolved gases;
- oxidizing or reducing conditions;
- cleaning and sanitizing agents;
- temporary commissioning fluids;
- mixtures and reaction products;
- permeation or swelling concerns.

Chemical resistance can change with stress, temperature, concentration, and time.

## 6.5 Multiphase and Non-Homogeneous Service

Examples include gas-liquid flow, liquid-solid slurry, gas-liquid-solid flow, foams, emulsions, suspensions, and crystallizing solutions.

These conditions can alter:

- pressure loss and flow regime;
- gas accumulation and venting;
- pump performance;
- erosion and particle settling;
- vibration and cyclic loading;
- instrumentation response;
- local heat and mass transfer.

Multiphase service should be defined explicitly rather than treated as a minor correction to single-phase design.

## 6.6 Time as a Design Variable

Thermoplastics exhibit time-dependent deformation and strength. The engineer should ask:

- How long will the system operate near maximum pressure or temperature?
- How many pressure and thermal cycles are expected?
- What is the duration of cleaning or upset exposure?
- Will sustained support, valve, or equipment loads relax or redistribute?
- How does the required design life affect allowable stress and inspection?

A peak condition without duration or frequency is incomplete design information.

## 6.7 Foreseeable Change

Facilities evolve through increased capacity, new equipment, replacement pumps, changed chemicals, revised schedules, and control modifications.

Not every future condition can be predicted, but foreseeable changes should be considered where practical. Design flexibility can include capacity margin, accessible connections, isolation, replaceable components, and documented limitations.

## 6.8 Representing the Envelope

The service envelope is multidimensional. Useful representations may include:

- pressure-temperature maps;
- operating-state tables;
- cycle histograms;
- chemical concentration-temperature matrices;
- load cases and combinations;
- transient scenarios;
- time-at-condition profiles.

The selected material and system design must be evaluated against the complete set, not one isolated maximum.

## 6.9 Common Mistakes

- designing only for nominal operation;
- combining unrelated maxima without understanding whether they can occur together;
- omitting short but frequent transients;
- ignoring cleaning and maintenance exposure;
- treating gas or solids as negligible;
- failing to define duration and cycle count;
- assuming future process changes are irrelevant;
- neglecting loads introduced by connected equipment and supports.

## Chapter Summary

The design envelope is the complete range and combination of conditions the system may credibly experience. It includes process, chemical, hydraulic, mechanical, environmental, and time-dependent factors.

A thermoplastic piping system should be selected and evaluated against this full envelope rather than a nominal operating point.
