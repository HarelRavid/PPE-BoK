# Chapter 7 — Engineering Risk and Uncertainty

## Why This Chapter Matters

Every piping system is designed under uncertainty. No engineer can know the exact future operating conditions, maintenance quality, process modifications, environmental changes, material variability, or human errors that will occur over decades of service.

The objective of engineering is therefore not to eliminate uncertainty. It is to understand it, reduce it where practical, and manage the associated risk responsibly.

## 7.1 Decision-Making Under Uncertainty

Engineering rarely produces absolute answers. It works with imperfect models, variable materials, manufacturing tolerances, incomplete operating histories, and changing requirements.

A sound decision is one that is reasonable, documented, and technically defensible based on the evidence available at the time—not one that claims impossible certainty.

## 7.2 Sources of Uncertainty

### Material variability

Properties vary between resin grades, batches, processing histories, dimensions, temperatures, and environments.

### Manufacturing and joining variability

Wall thickness, ovality, surface condition, fitting dimensions, heating, alignment, contamination, and workmanship are not perfectly identical.

### Process variability

Pressure, temperature, flow, composition, solids, and gas fraction change during normal and abnormal operation.

### Environmental variability

Ambient temperature, UV exposure, soil conditions, groundwater, chemical atmosphere, and external loads vary with location and time.

### Human and organizational variability

Installation, inspection, operation, maintenance, documentation, and management of change depend on people and systems.

### Model uncertainty

Calculations and correlations simplify reality. Their assumptions and validation range may not fully represent the actual system.

## 7.3 Variability Is Not Automatically Nonconformance

Engineering products are manufactured and operated within ranges. Standards specify tolerances because exact uniformity is impossible.

The key question is whether the observed variability remains within acceptable limits and whether its combined effect is accounted for in design and acceptance.

## 7.4 Risk and Uncertainty Are Different

Uncertainty is incomplete knowledge. Risk combines the likelihood of an event with its consequences.

Uncertain future cycle count may represent low or high risk depending on the system’s sensitivity to fatigue and the consequence of failure.

Reducing uncertainty improves risk assessment, but risk can also be reduced through prevention, detection, containment, isolation, redundancy, or operational controls.

## 7.5 Design Margins

Margins may arise from:

- code-defined design coefficients;
- long-term material classification;
- statistical characterization;
- dimensional tolerances;
- specified load combinations;
- uncertainty in service conditions;
- project-specific risk controls.

Margins should not be arbitrary. Excessive conservatism can create cost, stiffness, installation difficulty, and unintended load paths without proportional benefit. Insufficient margin can reduce reliability.

## 7.6 Robustness and Resilience

A robust design remains acceptable despite expected variability. A resilient system can tolerate and recover from disturbances without disproportionate consequences.

Examples of practical robustness include:

- avoiding geometries that concentrate stress;
- providing controlled thermal movement;
- supporting heavy components independently;
- enabling drainage and venting;
- selecting joints that can be produced and inspected reliably at the site;
- monitoring variables that indicate degradation;
- designing isolation and repair access.

## 7.7 Unknown Unknowns

Some events cannot be identified during design. The existence of unknowns does not justify speculation, but it does support conservative assumptions where consequences are severe, independent review, monitoring, learning from operating data, and effective management of change.

## 7.8 Engineering Judgement

Judgement combines:

- physical understanding;
- standards and accepted practice;
- test and operating evidence;
- recognition of model limits;
- project constraints;
- failure consequence;
- willingness to revise conclusions when evidence changes.

Judgement is not personal preference. It must be explainable and traceable.

## 7.9 Learning from Failure and Near Misses

Failures expose assumptions that were incorrect, incomplete, or no longer valid. Near misses and unexpected degradation can be equally valuable.

A useful investigation asks not only what failed, but:

- which decision or assumption allowed the mechanism to develop;
- why available controls did not prevent or detect it;
- whether the original decision was reasonable at the time;
- what systemic change will prevent recurrence.

## 7.10 Common Errors

- treating uncertainty as a reason to avoid a decision;
- hiding uncertainty behind precise numbers;
- applying safety factors without understanding what they cover;
- assuming code compliance eliminates risk;
- relying on previous success without comparing conditions;
- considering only probability and ignoring consequence;
- failing to update risk after modification or new evidence.

## Chapter Summary

Engineering is the practice of making defensible decisions despite incomplete knowledge. Uncertainty must be identified and documented; risk must be evaluated through likelihood and consequence; and controls must be selected according to the actual failure mechanisms and system context.

Good engineering manages uncertainty without pretending it has disappeared.
